Brief for Claude Design, version 2

Invites you text, replies that sort themselves

A mobile-first web app for sending an event invite by text and running the RSVPs and the day around it. This brief asks for a name, a visual identity, the key screens, six themes, design tokens, and a pass on the words. It includes what competitors look like and what is current in app design, so the work starts from the market rather than from a blank canvas.

The product

A host signs in with Google, creates an event in a few minutes, and adds guests. Every guest gets a personal link. The host texts the links from their own phone with one tap each, so the message arrives from someone the guest knows. The guest opens a page that greets them by name, sees the invite, taps yes or no, answers a couple of quick questions if the host asked (how many, dietary needs), and is done. The host watches replies arrive, chases the quiet ones, posts updates, runs a bring-a-plate list and a group gift, shares a runsheet for the day, sends a see-you-soon message the day before, and sends thank-you photos afterwards. Co-hosts share all of it.

Who it is for

HostsParents organising a kids' party, adults doing a birthday or housewarming, someone running a baby shower, farewell, family gathering or community event. On their phone, busy, not designers. Marcia's first hosts are her own clients and friends.
GuestsEveryone the host knows. All ages, every phone, no app, no account. One link in a chat, understood in three seconds.
Co-hostsThe other parent, a partner, a friend helping. Same screens as the host, from their own phone.
Special guests and organisersA speaker or helper who needs their part of the day; a guest running the group gift.

What competitors look like

Two families exist. The social apps (Partiful, Apple Invites, Luma) are page-first: a cover image, a black or white canvas, playful motion. The stationery apps (Paperless Post, Evite, Punchbowl, Greenvelope, InviteDrop) are card-first: an animated envelope, a designed card, a form underneath. Neither family is built around a host's own artwork, and none of them is warm and quiet at the same time.

The gap

Nobody owns "quiet, warm, host-first". Every competitor either shouts (gradients, stickers, envelopes) or reads as a tool (dashboards, forms). The space is a product that feels like a nicely set table: the host's invite is the centrepiece, and everything the app adds is the good plate underneath it.

What is current in app design

Where 2026 consumer apps have landed, and how it applies here. Use these as a checklist, not a style.

  • Intentional minimalism. Fewer controls, stronger hierarchy. Every element earns its place. For us: one primary action per screen, big yes and no, chips over dropdowns.
  • Micro-interactions as feedback, not decoration. A tap confirms with a small movement, a save shows a toast, an error nudges. For us: the yes tap should feel good once, the dashboard trail should animate in when a reply lands.
  • Bottom sheets and cards. Secondary actions live in a sheet near the thumb with a drag handle; content is organised as cards you can scan. For us: per-guest actions (Text, WhatsApp, Copy, New link, Merge) belong in a sheet, not a row of tiny buttons.
  • Progressive disclosure and conversational onboarding. Ask for one thing at a time, show the result early. For us: event setup as a short conversation with a live preview, and the RSVP as a chat-like sequence rather than a form.
  • Empty states that feel filled. A new host's first screen should show what the app does, not a blank list.
  • Glass and blur used surgically. A floating bar or sheet may use it; backgrounds should not.
  • Dark mode is expected. Phase 1 ships light only, but tokens should be dark-ready so it is a flip later, not a redesign.
  • Accessibility as default. 44 px targets, AA contrast, reduced-motion respected, no meaning carried by colour alone.

Anti-trends, please avoid

  • The purple-to-blue or purple-to-pink gradient hero. It is the visual signature of AI-made products now.
  • Inter or Space Grotesk with rounded cards and an accent rail. Reads as a template.
  • Emoji as section markers, everything centred, confetti everywhere.
  • Near-black canvases with one acid pop colour. Wrong for a kids' party invite.

Name brainstorm

The name has one job: it has to work in a text message. "I've sent you a [name]" or "reply on [name]" should sound natural to an Australian parent. Criteria:

  • One or two syllables, easy to say and spell after hearing it once.
  • Not RSVP jargon, not "vite" or "invit" (that corner is crowded: Evite, Invitd, Invyt, InviteDrop, Lemonvite, Invitify).
  • Warm, a little playful, never childish, since the same product does a 40th and a farewell.
  • Available as a .com.au or .au and ideally .app, with no existing invitation app of the same name. Marcia will run the final checks; flag anything you find.

Three visual directions to explore

Starting points, not answers. Show each one under a loud portrait Canva invite and under the text-only look, so we can judge how well the chrome steps back and how well it carries a page on its own.

Direction ASet tableWarm neutrals, a paper-like ground, a serious serif for titles with a plain sans for everything else, generous margins, one accent taken from the host's artwork. The app is the good plate under the invite. Risk: reads formal if the type gets too elegant.
Direction BBrisbane lightBright white, a warm sun-coloured accent by default, big rounded friendly type, chips and pills, small illustrated marks (a sun, a hat, a plate) used sparingly. Playful without being for kids. Risk: drifts toward Partiful if gradients creep in.

Whichever wins, the six gallery themes carry the personality (kids, playful, elegant, family, celebration) and the chrome stays consistent underneath.

Design principles

  1. The invite is the hero, and it is usually theirs.Chrome quiet enough to sit under any artwork in any colour. Accent is extracted from the upload, so every component must work in any hue and still pass contrast.
  2. Three looks, one layout.Text only, gallery theme, uploaded image. Only the top changes. Details, RSVP, calendar and extras are identical across the three.
  3. One tap wherever possible.Big yes and no, chips over dropdowns, steppers over number fields, nothing needs a keyboard unless the host asked a question.
  4. Feels like a message from a friend, not a form from a venue.Warm, short, host's voice.
  5. The dashboard is operated, not read.Counts and what-needs-doing above the fold. State shown as shape and colour, not just numbers.
  6. Phone first, laptop second.Every screen at 390 px first. Hosts do most of this on their phone, guests do all of it there.
  7. Kind to the nervous.Sending a text to forty parents is a small act of courage. The Send next flow should feel like being handed each message by a calm friend.

Screens and states

Priority order for the first round is the guest invite page, the RSVP conversation and the host dashboard, because they are the product. Everything else can follow in a second round. For every screen, please show:

  • The at-rest state with the example content (Leo's 6th, below).
  • The empty state (no guests yet, no replies yet, no updates yet) written so it teaches.
  • One error state (upload failed, link closed, wrong link).
  • Loading and saving feedback (skeleton or spinner, toast on save).

Iconography: a single consistent set, outline or duotone, never mixed with emoji in the chrome. Emoji may appear only where the host or guest typed them. Motion: one signature moment on the yes tap, otherwise 150 to 250 ms transitions, all respecting reduced-motion.

The wording pass

The journeys page ends with a table of every default line guests and hosts see. Those lines are approved in meaning, not in music. Go through all of them and propose better wording wherever it can sound nicer, warmer or clearer, keeping the meaning and roughly the length. Return a two-column table, current and proposed, with a short reason where it is not obvious.

Two we already suspect can improve:

  • "What we'll serve" as a label and its lines ("We'll have a BBQ going."). It reads like a menu board. Something that sounds like a host talking would be better.
  • Button labels throughout: Send my reply, Change my answer, I've chipped in, Got it, I'll keep the date free, Sorry, I can no longer make it.

House rules for copy

  • Australian English, spelling included.
  • Never use an em dash. Use a comma, full stop, colon or brackets.
  • One exclamation mark per line at most, never in error messages.
  • Written as the host speaking ("we"), never as the app speaking.
  • Signed off word for word, leave as they are: "We're keeping it to invited kids only. Sorry, siblings!" and "Parents welcome to stay." and "We would love to know if you can still make it :)"

Constraints and scope

  • Web app in Next.js. Design with components and tokens, not one-off pixels. Fonts on Google Fonts or licensed for web.
  • 390 px is the primary canvas. Tap targets at least 44 px. Contrast to WCAG AA on every accent hue, since the accent comes from the host's upload.
  • Uploaded invites are portrait more often than not. Show the layout coping with a tall A5 design and a square one.
  • No licensed characters or brands in themes or examples.
  • No ads, no watermarks, no "made with" badges on guest pages.
  • Light theme only in phase 1, tokens dark-ready.

Not in scope: payments, in-app text sending, automatic reminders, day-of check-in, checklist, waitlist, view-only links, CSV export, a marketing site beyond a single landing page.
